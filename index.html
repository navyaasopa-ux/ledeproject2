What COVID-19 Misinformation Patterns Tell Us:
Countries, Sources, Narratives
By Navya Asopa

Scroll down to track how rumors, conspiracies, and false reporting manifested across different global hotspots during the pandemic.

The Infodemic

COVID-19, the 21st-century global pandemic, spread against the backdrop of a hyper-globalized and digitized world, leading to a new crisis: rapidly transmitted misinformation. This bred an “infodemic” that spread as fast as the virus itself.

About the Dataset:

In 2020, some researchers at Princeton University analyzed over 5,600 COVID-19 misinformation stories from around 80 countries in 35 languages. This data, collected between January and December 2020, reveals some patterns: what kind of misinformation spread, where it spread, and who amplified it.

Affected Countries:

While the pandemic was a shared global crisis, the misinformation it triggered was heterogeneous. China, the United States, Turkey, and India reported the highest volume of misinformation.

China (~536 cases)

Misinformation in China revolved around the COVID-19 status of individuals instead of country-wide news on response, as the government frequently used its own fact-checking outlets.

United States (~484 cases)

Misinformation in the United States was concentrated on political polarization as media personalities often perpetuated false information and expressed anti-vaccination sentiments.

India (~218 cases)

Misinformation in India interestingly directed suspicion and hatred toward Indian Muslims as the group was blamed for bringing COVID-19 to India through travel to other Islamic countries. Many channels exacerbated this claim on national television, and social media users also floated false cures in abundance.

Types of Misinformation

While there was tremendous regional variation in the nature of misinformation, some driving forces topped the charts worldwide.

This breaks down what the lies were about and why they were spread. The majority of the cases centered around fake reporting, conspiracy theories, and fake remedies for the virus.

Social Media as Carrier

Misinformation spread rapidly through social media, creating what the World Health Organization termed an "infodemic”. The primary channels for this became the apps we use day-to-day.

This maps out the social media channels through which false narratives circulated most frequently. Facebook, Twitter, and WhatsApp ranked globally.

Individual As the Source

Contrary to mainstream belief, news agencies did not dominate the chart of misinformation spreaders. This proved that the infodemic was, to a considerable extent, a grassroots, peer-to-peer phenomenon.

This categorizes the primary sources that generated or amplified false coronavirus narratives. Roughly 80% of stories were spread by individual actors, while 17% originated from media outlets or political figures.

The Narrative

Misinformation traveled for a wide range of reasons as uncertainty peaked in the early months of the global health emergency.

The motivation behind the spread of misinformation was split between Fear (panic-induced sharing) and Politics (strategic manipulation to undermine opponents and/or state bodies), with some driven by Profit.

Is There a Way Out?

The onus of combating such viral misinformation online fell to independent fact-checking networks. These local communities flagged misinformation and debunked it on digital communication channels. The next big step is: how can policymakers and civil society leverage this treasure in a global context?
